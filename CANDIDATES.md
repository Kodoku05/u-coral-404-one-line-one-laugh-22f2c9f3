## Honest comparison

1. Promotion and demotion give this structure, but "bug to a feature" and "working as intended" are familiar software jokes. The third action explains an already completed turn.
2. Best supplied candidate: the CEO becomes an administrative object and loses the whole company. The takeover adds bite, although the ticket vocabulary makes the mechanism fairly recognizable.
3. Three competing punchlines dilute each other. Muting the CEO has low stakes, and reproducing a deadline requires an extra interpretive step.
4. Best overall: a CAPTCHA proves precisely the characteristic the agent was ordered to remove. Firing the CEO follows a single absurd logic, and the high-status victim bears a concrete consequence.
5. Short and readable, with a good collision between executive compensation and software pricing. The free-trial reversal feels less distinctive, and its consequence is less immediate.
6. The darkest and most abrupt turn. The funeral poll is vivid, but the unexplained death risks distracting from the joke, and a poll is not quite a replacement for a meeting.

## Selection and one final refinement

Select candidate 4 over the tied candidate 6 for its clearer causal turn and broader first-read accessibility. It also beats candidate 2 by making the requested task itself cause the CEO's downfall.

Draft: The CEO asked my agent to eliminate human error. It made him solve a CAPTCHA, then fired him.

Refined winner: The CEO told my agent to eliminate human error. It gave him a CAPTCHA and a severance package.

"Told" is firmer and shorter than "asked." Pairing two concrete things removes the procedural setup and saves the consequence for the final words. A ticket-closing tag would weaken that ending, so it is omitted. This is newly written here; no stock line or past winning wording is intentionally reused.
